"We need an AI agent" is one of the most common things I hear on discovery calls. Sometimes that's true. Often, what the business needs is simpler — and cheaper, faster and more reliable.

## The difference in one sentence

- An **automation** follows steps *you* define: when X happens, do Y, then Z.
- An **agent** is given a goal and tools, and *decides* which steps to take.

Both can use AI. The question is who's in charge of the plan: your process, or the model.

## When a plain automation wins

Choose automation when the steps are known and stable:

- New invoice arrives → extract fields with AI → validate → push to accounting → notify finance.
- Form submitted → classify urgency → route to the right team → send confirmation.

AI handles the fuzzy parts (reading a document, classifying a message), but the *flow* is deterministic. That makes it predictable, testable and easy to audit — which matters a lot in finance, healthcare and government work.

## When an agent earns its keep

Choose an agent when the path genuinely varies from case to case:

- A support assistant that may need to look up an order, check a policy, *or* escalate — depending on the conversation.
- A research assistant that searches several sources and decides when it has enough.
- An internal copilot that can query your database, draft a report and file a ticket.

Agents shine when enumerating every path up front would be impossible.

## The hybrid most businesses end up with

In practice, the best systems are **automations with agentic steps**: a reliable backbone you control, with an agent handling the one or two steps that need judgment. You get flexibility where it helps and guarantees where you need them.

## Questions I ask before building either

1. What happens today, step by step, and who does it?
2. What does a mistake cost — and who catches it?
3. Where does the data live, and is it accessible?
4. What does "done well" look like, in a number?
